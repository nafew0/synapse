# Research paper (IMRaD)

Use the target journal's author guide over everything here. Typical full paper: 5,000–8,000 words
(sciences and engineering), 7,000–10,000 (social sciences), excluding references. Figures below are
typical, not rules.

| Section | What goes in it | Typical length |
|---|---|---|
| Title | The variables or phenomenon, the population or context, the design if it matters. No "novel", no question marks unless the journal likes them. | 10–20 words |
| Abstract | Background (1 sentence), aim, method (design, sample, analysis), key results with numbers, conclusion and implication. Structured if the journal asks. | 150–300 words |
| Keywords | 4–6 terms not already in the title. | — |
| 1. Introduction | Problem and why it matters; what is known (cited); the specific gap; aim, objectives or hypotheses; contribution in one or two sentences. | 10–15% |
| 2. Literature review / Theoretical framework | Only when the field expects a separate section. Themes, not paper-by-paper summaries; ends in the hypotheses or framework. | 10–20% |
| 3. Materials and methods | Design, setting, participants or data, sampling and size justification, instruments with validity and reliability, procedure, analysis (software and version), ethics approval with reference number. Enough to replicate. | 15–20% |
| 4. Results | Findings in the order of the objectives; tables and figures referenced in text; effect sizes and confidence intervals, not p-values alone. No interpretation. | 20–25% |
| 5. Discussion | Main finding first; compare with prior work (cited); explanations; theoretical and practical implications; limitations; future work. | 20–25% |
| 6. Conclusion | Answer to the research question; contribution; one line on implication. No new results, no citations. | 150–300 words |
| Declarations | Funding, conflicts of interest, data availability, author contributions (CRediT), ethics, use of AI tools if the journal asks. | — |
| References | Only works cited; the journal's style. | — |

## Building a paper from given papers ("new novel research")

1. Summarise each supplied paper in one line: question, method, finding, stated limitation.
2. Derive the gap from those limitations and future-work sections; quote where it came from.
3. Propose the question, design and analysis. Do not write a Results section with numbers for a
   study that has not been run: write the planned analysis, or leave `[result]` placeholders.
4. Novelty: "Among the supplied papers, none examines X in Y" is a supported claim; "this is the
   first study" is not, unless a search was done and is described.

## Finalising for a journal (e.g. Q1)

Check against the author guide: word limit, abstract structure and length, section names, reference
style, figure resolution, declarations, highlights or graphical abstract, cover letter. Report what
was checked; never call it "submission-ready" without that list.

## Common reviewer complaints

- Gap not clearly stated, or contribution overstated ("first", "novel" with no support).
- Introduction is a literature dump; objectives do not match the results.
- Methods not replicable: sampling, instrument validation or analysis details missing.
- Results restate every table cell; discussion repeats results without comparison to literature.
- Limitations missing or token; conclusions go beyond the data (causal language for correlational data).
- Outdated or self-heavy references; citations missing from the reference list or the reverse.
- Language: long sentences, inconsistent terms and abbreviations, mixed British and American spelling.
