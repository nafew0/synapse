# Thesis chapters

Follow the university's thesis guide when the user has one (chapter names, numbering, word limit).
Typical PhD thesis 60,000–100,000 words; MPhil 40,000–60,000; master's 15,000–25,000.

## Chapter 1. Introduction (8–12% of the thesis)

- 1.1 Background: the field and the problem, narrowing to the context (e.g. Sri Lanka, a sector).
- 1.2 Problem statement: what is wrong or unknown, with evidence (cited or the user's data).
- 1.3 Research questions and objectives: one general objective, 3–5 specific ones; each answerable.
- 1.4 Significance: who benefits and how (theory, practice, policy).
- 1.5 Scope and delimitations; 1.6 definitions of key terms; 1.7 outline of the thesis.

## Chapter 2. Literature review (20–30%)

- Organise by themes or constructs, then the theoretical framework, then the conceptual framework
  (figure with variables and hypotheses).
- End with an explicit **research gap** paragraph: what is known, what is not (contextual,
  methodological, theoretical or population gap), and how this study addresses it. One gap per
  paragraph, each tied to cited work. See `literature-review.md`.

## Chapter 3. Methodology (10–15%)

Typical subsections; rename to the university's scheme.

- 3.1 Research philosophy and approach: paradigm (positivist, interpretivist, pragmatist) and
  deductive or inductive approach, justified for this question.
- 3.2 Research design: quantitative, qualitative or mixed methods (state the mixed design:
  convergent, explanatory or exploratory sequential); cross-sectional or longitudinal.
- 3.3 Population, sample and sampling: population size and source, sampling technique, sample size
  and how it was determined (formula, power analysis, saturation). Use the user's figures only.
- 3.4 Data collection: instruments (adapted from whom, items, scale), pilot test, validity and
  reliability (Cronbach's alpha, CFA, triangulation, member checking), procedure.
- 3.5 Data analysis: techniques per objective (a table objective → data → analysis works well),
  software and version; ethical considerations (approval body and number, consent, anonymity).

## Chapter 4. Results (15–25%)

Organise by objective or hypothesis. Descriptives, then tests; tables numbered and referred to.
Qualitative: themes with participant quotes (only real quotes from the user's data).

## Chapter 5. Discussion (15–20%)

Each finding against prior literature: agrees, disagrees, why. Implications for theory and practice.

## Chapter 6. Conclusion and recommendations (5–8%)

Answer each objective in turn; contributions; recommendations tied to findings; limitations;
further research.

## Common examiner complaints

- Objectives, questions, hypotheses and analysis do not line up one to one.
- Literature review summarises papers one by one; the gap is asserted, not shown.
- Methodology names a philosophy without justifying it; sample size not justified.
- Reliability and validity claimed without figures.
- Discussion does not return to the literature; recommendations not grounded in findings.
