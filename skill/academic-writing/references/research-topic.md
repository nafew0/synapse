# Research topic and title

## A good topic

- **Specific**: names the variables or phenomenon, the population, and the context.
- **Gap-based**: comes from a stated gap (supplied papers, a search, or the user's observation), not
  from what "sounds new".
- **Feasible**: data, access, time, ethics and skills fit the degree or grant.
- **Significant**: someone (theory, practice, policy) is better off with the answer.
- **Researchable**: answerable with evidence; not a yes/no opinion question.

## Title patterns

- Relationship: "The effect of X on Y among Z in [context]"
- Mediation or moderation: "The mediating role of M in the relationship between X and Y: evidence from [context]"
- Design-led: "A mixed-methods study of X in [context]"
- Development: "Design and evaluation of X for Y"
- Typical length 10–20 words; avoid "A study of", "novel", undefined abbreviations.

## Output format: three alternatives

For each alternative:

1. **Title**
2. **Gap it addresses** (1–2 lines; cite the supplied paper or mark `[citation needed: ...]`)
3. **Research question(s)** (1–3)
4. **Suggested design and data** (1–2 lines)
5. **Feasibility note** (data access, time, risks)

Then one line recommending one and why.

## Novelty checks

A novelty judgement needs a search. With a search tool: search the main databases or the web with
2–3 query strings, report what was searched, the closest works found and how the topic differs, and
say the check is not exhaustive. Without one: give the queries the user should run (Google Scholar,
Scopus, Web of Science), and say novelty is unconfirmed. Never say "no one has studied this" from
memory.

## Classifying titles into categories

Use only the categories the user gave. Return a table: title → category → one-line reason. Mark
titles that fit two categories or none, rather than forcing them.
